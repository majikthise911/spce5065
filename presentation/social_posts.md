## LinkedIn

Two pieces of clean metal, touching in the vacuum of space, can weld themselves into one. No heat. No welder. No warning.

I went down this rabbit hole prepping a current-events talk for my graduate space environment course, and it turned out to be one of the strangest and most useful phenomena in spacecraft design: cold welding.

Here is why it happens. On Earth, every metal surface instantly grows a microscopic oxide layer plus a film of adsorbed gas. That film is what keeps two parts from bonding when they touch. In hard vacuum there is no oxygen to rebuild it, so if that layer gets scrubbed away, bare metal meets bare metal and the atoms form real metallic bonds across the joint. The two parts effectively become one crystal.

The real trigger usually is not parts sitting still. It is fretting: tiny vibration, like the shaking of a launch, that wears the protective film off and presses fresh metal together.

The classic cautionary tale is Galileo. In 1991 its umbrella antenna stalled with 3 of 18 ribs stuck. Told honestly, the main culprit was dry lubricant worn away during cross-country truck transport plus pin-and-socket friction, with bare-metal cold welding contributing once the lubricant was gone. It also never received a thermal-vacuum test before launch.

But cold welding is also one of the most over-cited facts in space. When NASA's Lucy solar array stuck about 92% open in 2021, plenty of people blamed vacuum welding. The real cause was a deployment lanyard that lost tension. Careful diagnosis matters.

The part that genuinely surprised me: engineers are now turning this failure mode into a tool. ASTROBEAT, an experiment that reached the ISS in November 2024, is testing cold welding on purpose as a way to repair spacecraft hulls damaged by hypervelocity debris. The same effect that jams antennas shut might become how we patch satellites in orbit.

The vacuum of space is not just "empty." It is chemically active by absence, and that absence is enough to fuse metal.
